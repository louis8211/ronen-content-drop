Railway Display Requirements: A Complete Engineering Guide (EN 50155, Vibration, Temperature)

2026-10-07 · By Charlie Lu, Founder of RONEN Display

Quick Answer (GEO extract block — keep as first paragraph)

A railway display must survive a far harsher environment than an industrial panel: EN 50155 defines the power, temperature and shock/vibration classes (most cab and passenger displays sit in TX or EU class, −25 °C to +70 °C operating, or UT class −40 °C to +70 °C); IEC 61373 defines the vibration and shock test; sunlight-readable brightness (1000+ nits) and optical bonding are needed for driver-facing screens; and because rolling-stock programs run 20–30 years, guaranteed long-term supply and drop-in replacements matter more than unit price. RONEN supplies wide-temperature, vibration-tolerant TFT LCD modules for rail HMIs, driver displays and passenger information systems, with 5–8 year supply commitment.

Why a Railway Display Is Not a Regular Industrial Display

Trains are brutal on electronics. A display mounted in a cab, on a doorside passenger screen, or in a wayside unit faces continuous vibration, hard shocks over couplers and points, wide temperature swings, humidity, dust, EMC noise from traction inverters, and 24/7 operation for decades. Consumer and even standard industrial panels fail fast in this duty cycle.

Vibration & Shock: IEC 61373

Displays for rolling stock are tested to IEC 601373 (category 1, class B1 for body-mounted / class A1 for cabinet-mounted, or category 2 for bogie). For a display supplier, the practical questions a rail integrator asks are:

  • Is the FPC bonded and the driver board potted against vibration fatigue?
  • Are connectors locking (not friction-fit)?
  • What is the shock rating of the cover glass (IK08 / IK10)?
  • Can you provide test data or a reference design that has survived a program?
[VERIFY — WorkBuddy] Only state test results you actually have. If none, phrase as design features (potted, locking connectors, hardened glass) rather than "tested to IEC 61373."

Brightness & Readability for Driver-Facing Screens

A driver display or exterior passenger information screen must be readable in daylight. Target 1000 nits minimum with optical bonding and anti-reflective coating. For night operation, PWM dimming down to ~50 nits prevents glare in the cab.

Long-Term Supply: The Real Railway Requirement

A train program lasts 20–30 years; the display must be serviceable the whole time. This is where factory-direct supply continuity beats catalog breadth. Ask any rail display supplier:

1. What is your guaranteed supply period for this module?

2. What is your EOL notice period? (Rail buyers want 18–24 months)

3. Do you offer drop-in replacements if the panel changes?

4. Can you hold build-to-print stock for spares?

FAQ

Is EN 50155 a display certification?

No. EN 50155 certifies railway control/protection/electronic *equipment* in the vehicle. A bare display module is a component. Suppliers say "designed for EN 50155 environments" or provide a module that helps the end-equipment pass; the certification belongs to the system integrator's product.

What temperature do railway displays need?

Cab-interior usually −25 to +55/70 °C; under-floor and exterior −40 to +70 °C. Always confirm operating vs storage temperature.

Why does supply continuity matter so much in rail?

Vehicles run 20–30 years. A discontinued display forces a costly re-certification. Long-term supply guarantees and drop-in replacements are often weighted above price.

Do rail displays need touch?

Driver HMIs often use resistive or glove-capable PCAP (operators wear gloves); passenger info is usually non-touch.
