### Choosing by interface, not by size

800 × 480 is the resolution where three interface families still coexist, and picking the wrong one costs you a converter board:

- **RGB parallel (16/20/24-bit)** — still the majority of this resolution. It needs an LCD controller in the host (classic STM32 F4/F7 with LTDC, some embedded SoCs) and a frame buffer. Simple timing, no bridge chip, but it eats GPIOs and needs careful signal termination above ~25 MHz pixel clock.
- **MIPI-DSI** — for modern application processors (i.MX 8/9, RK35xx, Allwinner, ESP32-P4 class). Fewer lanes, better EMI behaviour at the same bandwidth. See our driver-IC cross-reference notes in `/blog/tft-driver-controller-ic-guide`.
- **LVDS** — mostly 7″ and larger, dual-channel on some parts. If your board already has an HDMI/DP source, you need a bridge; check pin order before you assume drop-in.

Use the **aspect-ratio calculator** (`/tools/aspect-ratio-calculator`) to confirm the active area fits your bezel, and the **replacement finder** (`/tools/lcd-replacement-finder`) if you are matching an obsolete panel.

### Choosing by brightness

| Panel brightness | Realistic use | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 350–500 cd/m² | Indoor HMI, control cabinets | Fine behind clear glass |
| 700–900 cd/m² | Retail, factory floor with daylight | Measure at the eye, not at the panel |
| 1,000 cd/m²+ | Outdoor kiosks, marine, vehicle dash | Bonding and tint cut output; `[VERIFY]` the after-bonding figure per project |

Once you laminate a touch sensor or an anti-reflective cover glass, you lose a meaningful share of the panel's rated luminance — the exact number depends on the stack, so we state it per project rather than publishing a generic figure.

### Touch and thickness

RTP adds a spacer layer and typically ~1.1 mm over the bare module; CTP with optical bonding adds more, and changes the reading through gloves. Our catalogue lists per-model touch type and outline thickness, so compare outline depth against your enclosure before you fix the stack — that is where 800 × 480 builds usually go wrong, not on the electronics.

### If you are replacing an obsolete panel

Resolution alone is not compatibility. Match outline, active area, FPC exit direction, connector pinout, backlight voltage and interface family. Our **original module cross-reference zone** (`/landings/original-module-cross-reference-index`) lists which factory panels we have compared spec-by-spec, and anything not marked verified is labelled as engineering reference only — we do not claim drop-in interchangeability without evidence.

### The number on the sheet is not the number at the eye

Panel luminance is measured at full white, at room temperature, with no stack in front of it. Add a touch sensor, a anti-glare filter and a tinted cover glass and the delivered luminance drops. Ask for the **measured value behind your actual stack**, not the catalogue figure — a supplier who quotes 1,000 nits and never mentions the stack has not built many outdoor displays.

### What high brightness actually costs

- **Power and heat.** More backlight current means a warmer module; LED lifetime falls with junction temperature, and a sealed outdoor enclosure has nowhere to dump that heat.
- **Derating.** We specify brightness at a stated temperature; ask for the derating curve for your enclosure's internal temperature rather than trusting the 25 °C figure.
- **Optical design.** Reaching 1,000+ cd/m² in a thin outline usually means a denser light guide and tighter tolerances on the reflector, which is why high-brightness parts are not simply "the same panel, driven harder".
- **Dimming.** Sunlight-readable does not mean running flat out: without PWM dimming your display blinds the operator at night and burns power. See the backlight dimming discussion in `/blog/tft-backlight-led-lifetime-dimming-guide`.

### Matching the module to the environment

For marine and vehicle dashboards, brightness comes second to temperature range and condensation control — check the −30…+80 °C list below and our humid/temperature cycling failure notes (`/blog/humid-heat-backlight-fog-corrosion`, `/blog/temp-cycle-display-failure`). For self-service kiosks, the bigger risk is polariser degradation from UV, which is a cover-glass and film question, not a nit question.

Use `/tools/lcd-brightness-calculator` to sanity-check the target luminance against your enclosure and glass stack.

### What "wide temperature" is made of

Four things move when a panel is rated for −30 °C, and a supplier who changes only the datasheet text has changed none of them:

1. **Liquid crystal formulation.** Low-temperature nematic mixes keep viscosity down so pixels switch at usable speed. Standard mixes slow to hundreds of milliseconds in the cold, which shows up as ghosting on a dashboard, not on a bench at 25 °C.
2. **Polariser and sealants.** Adhesives stiffen and can delaminate through thermal cycling; this is the most common field failure we see discussed in `/blog/temp-cycle-display-failure`.
3. **Backlight LED bin and derating.** Output falls with temperature in both directions, and driving harder in the cold shortens life. Ask for the curve, not the 25 °C number.
4. **Driver IC grade.** The controller's own temperature rating must match or exceed the module's; that is why two panels with identical glass can carry different grades.

### The cold-start question nobody asks until integration

Below roughly −20 °C many panels need a warm-up period before the display is stable, and heaters cost power you may not have on a battery product. Decide early: preheat and accept power cost, or accept slower first-frame response and tune the UI for it. Our implementation notes are in `/blog/wide-temperature-lcd-how-to`, and storage/shelf-life handling in `/blog/lcd-panel-storage-shelf-life`.

### Certification, stated honestly

Our quality system is certified to **ISO 9001:2015 (TÜV SÜD)**. Bare display modules are not separately CE- or FCC-marked — those apply to the end product, and any supplier telling you a bare panel "has CE" is describing a self-declaration at best. RoHS and REACH statements are declarations of substance compliance based on component supplier data; test reports on request. `[VERIFY]` which documents we can produce today for a specific customer file.

### Shape claims: read the product page, not the resolution

240 × 240 tells you the pixel grid, not the glass. Some 240 × 240 parts are round, some are square. We label shape explicitly in the specification table ("IPS Round", "IPS Square"), and that field is what our own image-matching guard uses to decide which picture belongs on which page — so if a listing looks ambiguous, ask and we will confirm from the drawing rather than guess.

### Designing for a round active area

- The four corners of a square framebuffer are outside a circular active area. Reserve the safe area before you design the UI, and put status text on the horizontal diameter.
- Touch panels for round modules need a bonding margin; a sensor cut to the module outline leaves an unresponsive rim. State the bezel overlap you can accept.
- Small round parts usually use SPI or MCU interfaces with no frame buffer, which constrains colour depth and refresh. Driver IC cross-referencing is covered in `/blog/tft-driver-controller-ic-guide`.
- Check the mechanical envelope against your lens barrel: the 3.4″ 800 × 800 round part has a 99.60 × 99.79 mm outline, so it fits a ~100 mm housing, not a 90 mm one.
